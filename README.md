# Engaging Verifiers and Employers

Making credentials count outside the issuing organisation.

## About this course

**Course ID:** ICB-P17
**Framework alignment:** ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill ECOS "Ecosystem and recognition partnerships", VRITE pillar: Value, level L3 "Enable", stage A "Ability".

At this level you engage verifiers to agree how specific credentials are recognised, and you run pilot recognition arrangements. At the Ability stage you practise this on realistic tasks, in a controlled setting such as a case or a project.

**Who this course is for**

- Programme and micro-credential leads (E1)
- Professional bodies and sector associations (P1)
- L&D and corporate academy leads (C1)

**Prerequisite:** ICB-P17 builds on ICB-F04 "Checking a Digital Credential: What Verification Proves". You should know what a verification check does and does not tell a verifier before you start.

**Learning outcomes**

After this course you can:

1. Identify and prioritise verifiers for a credential.
2. Run verifier interviews to find out what evidence they need.
3. Co-design or adapt credentials with verifiers.
4. Set up a pilot recognition arrangement with clear terms.

Nothing is graded automatically inside this file.

## Mapping verifiers

**In this module you will**

- define what a verifier is and who counts as one,
- build a list of possible verifiers for one credential,
- rank them by influence and interest,
- choose a small group to engage first.

### Who is a verifier?

A verifier is a person or organisation that checks a credential and then makes a decision based on it. The decision may be to shortlist a candidate, grant credit, admit someone to a programme, allow someone to work on a task or give someone access to a role.

Verifiers are not all the same. Four kinds appear again and again.

- **Employers and hiring managers.** They decide whether a credential helps them judge a candidate. In many organisations the recruiter, the hiring manager and the HR system each play a different part.
- **Internal talent teams.** In a large organisation, a talent or mobility team may check credentials earned inside the company to place people in new roles.
- **Education providers.** Admissions teams and registrars decide whether a credential earns credit or entry.
- **Associations and regulators.** A professional body may accept a credential as evidence towards membership or a licence to practise. A regulator may set rules that no single credential can change.

In ICoBC course colours, employers are shown in orange, education providers in cyan and associations and policy bodies in lime. Earners, who carry the credential, are navy.

From ICB-F04 you know that a verification check can show who issued a credential and whether it has been changed or revoked. It does not tell a verifier whether the credential is right for their decision. That is why you need to talk to verifiers. Verification is a technical check. Recognition is a human decision, and you influence it by understanding what the decision-maker needs.

Skills-based hiring and internal mobility are widely discussed, and practice varies from one organisation to the next. Credentials can provide the evidence layer, but each verifier decides for themselves how much weight to give that evidence. Do not assume that interest in skills means a particular verifier will accept your credential.

### Listing the possible verifiers

Start with one credential and write down its purpose in a sentence. For example: "Shows that an earner can run a safe forklift shift handover, assessed by observation." Then ask four questions.

1. Who makes decisions about people who might hold this credential?
2. Who sets the rules those decision-makers follow?
3. Who advises or influences those decision-makers?
4. Who would be affected if the credential were accepted or rejected?

Work in specific roles and names of teams, not in broad labels. "Employers" is too broad to act on. "Warehouse operations managers at regional distribution centres" is something you can contact.

Include verifiers that are unlikely but possible. A first list of 10 to 20 entries is normal. Cut it down later.

For each entry, note what decision the verifier makes and how they would see the credential: through a link, a PDF, a profile, a conversation or a database. This tells you what effort a verifier would need to make, and effort affects recognition.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

**Live element (about 45 minutes).** Ask each participant to state their credential in one sentence and name three possible verifiers. Pair participants so that each pair challenges the other's list: "Which of these would actually use it?" Collect common verifier types on a flipchart.

**Watch for:** broad labels such as "industry" or "employers". Ask participants to name a role, a team and the decision that role makes.

</details>

### Prioritising by influence and interest

A long list is not a plan. You have limited time, so engage the verifiers who matter most first. A common tool is a map with two axes.

- **Influence:** how much the verifier's decision changes whether the credential is recognised. A professional body that controls entry to a licence has high influence. A single recruiter at a small firm has lower influence for a regional credential.
- **Interest:** how much the verifier cares about the topic or has a problem the credential could solve. A talent team that struggles to evidence skills has high interest.

Place each verifier in one of four areas.

- High influence, high interest: engage first, closely and often. These are your partners.
- High influence, lower interest: keep satisfied. Find out what would make the credential relevant to them.
- Lower influence, high interest: keep informed. They may become advocates.
- Lower influence, lower interest: monitor.

Your placement is a judgement, not a fact. Write the reason for each placement in one line, so that others can challenge it. Revisit the map after your first conversations, because your assumptions will change.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: Mapping verifiers for an internal mobility credential**

Meridian Foods runs a corporate academy. Its learning lead, Amara, has designed a micro-credential called "Line leader readiness". It certifies that an employee has completed a practical assessment in handling shift handovers, quality checks and basic coaching. Amara wants the credential to count when employees apply for team leader roles, both inside Meridian Foods and at supplier firms.

She states the credential's purpose in one sentence and then lists possible verifiers by role.

- Plant managers at four Meridian Foods sites (they appoint team leaders).
- The central HR talent team (it runs internal vacancies).
- The works council representative for production (it is consulted on role criteria).
- Hiring managers at two supplier firms.
- The regional food industry association (it runs a leadership programme).
- A local college that offers a supervisor qualification.

She places them on the map. Plant managers and the talent team are high influence and high interest, because vacancies are hard to fill. The works council is high influence and moderate interest, so she plans to keep it satisfied. The supplier hiring managers have lower influence over internal decisions and uncertain interest, so she plans to keep them informed. The industry association is lower influence on hiring but could become an advocate. The college is lower priority for now.

She writes one line of reasoning for each placement and marks two as "to test in interviews". She chooses four verifiers to engage first: two plant managers, the talent team lead and the works council representative.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Ask participants what they would change on Amara's map and why. A good discussion point is whether the works council belongs higher on interest. There is no single right answer. What matters is that every placement has a written reason.

</details>

**Practice activity: Build your verifier map**

Choose one credential for the whole course. Then:

1. Write the credential's purpose in one sentence.
2. List at least 10 possible verifiers, named by role or team, not by broad label.
3. For each, write the decision they make about earners.
4. Place each verifier on an influence and interest map. You can draw it by hand or in a table. Write one line of reasoning per verifier.
5. Select 4 to 6 verifiers to engage first. Explain your choice in three sentences.
6. Mark which placements are assumptions you will test in Module 2.

Keep this as the first part of your evidence: the **verifier map**.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

**Live element (about 40 minutes).** Participants swap maps in pairs. The reviewer asks three questions: Who is missing? Which placement has the weakest reason? Which verifier would you contact first, and why? Allow 10 minutes for revision afterwards.

</details>

## Verifier research

**In this module you will**

- plan a verifier interview with a clear question,
- run interview conversations that find out what evidence a verifier needs,
- avoid common mistakes such as leading questions,
- summarise findings in a form that others can use.

### Planning the research

Verifier research is a set of short, structured conversations. Its purpose is to find out how a verifier makes a decision and what evidence they need to feel confident. It is not a sales pitch for your credential.

Begin with a research question. Examples:

- "What evidence do plant managers use today when they appoint a team leader?"
- "What would an admissions team need to see before granting credit for a short course?"

Next, decide who to interview. Use your verifier map. Aim for 3 to 5 interviews with different verifiers. If you can, include at least two kinds of verifier, such as a hiring manager and a talent team lead, because their needs often differ.

Then arrange the practical details.

- **Invitation.** Say who you are, why you want to talk, how long it takes (30 minutes is a good length) and what you will do with the notes. Make clear that you are asking for advice and are not asking for a commitment.
- **Consent.** Ask permission to take notes or to record. Tell the person how you will store and use what they say. Follow your organisation's data protection policy. <!-- VERIFY: Check your organisation's own rules on recording interviews and storing personal data, and any local legal requirements, before running interviews. -->
- **Roles.** If possible, have one person lead and one person take notes.

Prepare a short guide with 6 to 8 open questions. You need not follow it in order, but it keeps you on topic.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

**Live element (about 30 minutes).** Participants draft a research question and an invitation message. In pairs, they swap and tighten the invitation until it fits in five sentences. Check that no invitation promises recognition.

</details>

### Asking good questions

Good interview questions are open, neutral and about real past behaviour. People are poor at predicting what they would do, but they can describe what they did last time.

Useful patterns:

- **Ask about the last time.** "Tell me about the last time you filled a team leader role. What did you look at?"
- **Ask about evidence.** "What do you look at to decide whether someone can do this? What do you trust most? What do you trust least?"
- **Ask about friction.** "What slows you down when you check someone's background?"
- **Ask about risk.** "What would make you reject a credential you had been shown?"
- **Ask about format.** "How would you want to see this information: in a document, a link, inside your system?"
- **Ask about decision rights.** "Who else needs to agree before this changes?"

Avoid these mistakes:

- **Leading questions.** "Wouldn't a badge like this be useful?" invites a polite yes.
- **Pitching.** If you spend the interview explaining your credential, you learn nothing.
- **Closed questions.** Yes or no answers give little to work with.
- **Hypotheticals.** "Would you ever use something like this?" gives unreliable answers.

Listen more than you talk. Use short prompts such as "Can you say more about that?" and "What happened next?" Leave pauses. People often add the most useful detail after a short silence.

In your notes, keep what was said separate from your interpretation. Write quotes or close paraphrases in one column and your own thoughts in another.

If a verifier says "we do not use credentials", that is a finding. Ask what they use instead and what it does well. Your credential may need to fit into an existing practice, or it may not be a fit at all.

### Summarising what you learned

After each interview, write a short note on the same day. Then bring the notes together into an interview summary. A useful summary has these parts:

1. **Purpose and method:** your research question, who you interviewed (roles, not personal names unless agreed) and how many.
2. **Decisions:** what decision each verifier makes and how often.
3. **Evidence needs:** what evidence they use now, what they trust and what they distrust.
4. **Barriers:** what could stop recognition, such as process, policy, cost or lack of awareness.
5. **Patterns and differences:** what several verifiers said, and where they disagreed.
6. **Implications:** what your credential, its evidence and its presentation may need to change.
7. **Open questions:** what you still do not know and who could answer it.

Keep the summary to two pages. Mark each point as "heard from several", "heard from one" or "your inference". Do not claim a pattern based on one conversation.

Treat quotes with care. Use short quotes only with the interviewee's permission, and do not attribute them to a named person without agreement.

Finally, check whether the findings change your verifier map. If the hiring manager has little say and a talent team makes the call, move them on the map.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: Interviewing admissions staff about credit for short courses**

Northvale College offers a 30-hour micro-credential called "Data handling for health care assistants". Its programme lead, Tomasz, hopes that a nearby university will grant credit towards a degree for it. He lists the university's admissions officer and the head of the nursing programme as high-priority verifiers.

His research question is: "What would the university need to see before it grants credit for a short course taken elsewhere?"

He arranges two 30-minute interviews. He starts the first with the question: "Tell me about the last time you assessed prior learning from another provider. What did you look at?" The admissions officer describes a checklist: the awarding body, the level, the number of hours, the learning outcomes and the way assessment was done. She says she does not trust attendance certificates, because they say nothing about what the person can do. She also says that a faculty board must approve any new category of credit, and that this happens twice a year.

Tomasz notes what he heard in one column and his own thoughts in another. His thought is: "The assessment method may matter more than the badge design." He resists the urge to pitch the course. At the end he asks, "Who else should I speak to?" and gets the name of the faculty board secretary.

In his summary he marks "assessment method is the key evidence" as heard from one verifier, and "board approval needed" as heard from one verifier. He plans a second interview with the head of nursing to test both points. He also moves the faculty board higher on his map.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

Ask participants what Tomasz did well and what else he could have asked. Possible answers: he asked about the last time, he asked what the verifier distrusts, he asked who else to talk to. He could have asked about cost, timing and how credit is recorded in student systems.

</details>

**Practice activity: Run and summarise verifier interviews**

Using your credential and verifier map from Module 1:

1. Write a research question and a guide of 6 to 8 open questions.
2. Write a short invitation message (five sentences or fewer).
3. Run 3 to 5 interviews. If real verifiers are not available, role-play with colleagues or fellow participants who take the part of verifiers and answer from their real working experience. Record clearly in your summary which interviews were role-played.
4. After each interview, write a same-day note with facts and interpretation in separate columns.
5. Write a two-page interview summary using the seven parts listed above. Label each finding as "heard from several", "heard from one" or "your inference".
6. Update your verifier map based on what you learned.

Keep the summary as the second part of your evidence: the **interview summary**.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

**Live element (about 60 minutes).** Run a role-play round. In groups of three, one participant interviews, one plays the verifier using a brief you provide, and one observes with a checklist: open questions asked, leading questions avoided, evidence needs uncovered. Rotate roles so everyone interviews once. Debrief with the group on the most common mistakes.

For the verifier brief, give the role-player a one-paragraph description of a realistic decision-maker and two or three hidden concerns that the interviewer can only discover by asking good questions.

</details>

## Co-design

**In this module you will**

- explain what co-design with verifiers means and where it stops,
- decide which parts of a credential are open to change,
- run a co-design cycle with verifiers,
- record agreed changes and the evidence behind them.

### What co-design means

Co-design means working with verifiers to shape or adapt a credential so that it gives them the evidence they need. You are not handing over control. The issuer stays responsible for the credential's quality and integrity. Verifiers contribute knowledge about the decision the credential must support.

Co-design can take several forms.

- **Adapting an existing credential.** You keep the core but change how it is described, assessed, evidenced or presented.
- **Designing a new credential with a partner.** For example, an employer and a college design a micro-credential around a shared skills need.
- **Mapping to a common reference.** You and the verifier agree how the credential relates to a skills framework, job profile or qualification level that the verifier already uses.

Before you begin, decide what is open to change and what is not. Fixed points often include the assessment standards, the quality assurance process and any rules of an awarding body or regulator. Open points often include the level of detail in the description, the examples of evidence, the way results are shared and the way skills are labelled.

A useful credential gives a verifier something they can use in their decision. In your interviews you will probably have found some of the following.

- **Clear claims.** What can the earner do, or what has the earner shown?
- **Assessment information.** How was it assessed, by whom and to what standard?
- **Evidence behind the claim.** A sample of work, an observation record or a project result.
- **Issuer information.** Who issued it and under what quality arrangements?
- **Currency.** When was it earned and does it expire?
- **Alignment.** How does it relate to roles, levels or skills the verifier already uses?

A credential cannot promise that its holder will perform well in every job. It gives evidence about what was assessed in a given setting. Co-design should help the verifier see that evidence clearly and should not exaggerate it.

Skills-based hiring and internal mobility are widely discussed, and practice varies. Do not assume that a verifier already works with skills data. If they do not, co-design may be about making the credential readable in the way the verifier already works, such as a job description or a competency list.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

**Live element (about 30 minutes).** Ask participants to list for their own credential what is fixed and what is open to change. Ask: "If a verifier asked for this change, would you accept, negotiate or refuse? Why?" Collect two or three borderline cases for group discussion.

</details>

### Running a co-design cycle

A co-design cycle is short and repeatable. One round can take a few days to a few weeks.

1. **Share findings.** Bring back what you heard in interviews. Check that you understood correctly.
2. **Frame the design question.** For example: "How can the credential show the assessment method to admissions staff in one page?"
3. **Generate options.** Sketch two or three versions together. Use rough drafts, not polished designs. A one-page mock-up of a credential description is enough.
4. **Test with the verifier.** Ask them to use the draft on a realistic case: "Here is a sample earner. Would this give you what you need to decide?" Watch what they look at and what they ask.
5. **Decide and record.** Agree what changes, who does what and by when. Note what you declined and why.
6. **Return to step 1** with the revised version if needed.

Invite a small group of verifiers, not a large committee. Three to six participants usually work well. Include at least one who is sceptical, since a sceptic finds weaknesses early.

Keep a **change log**. For each change, record the request, who made it, the evidence behind it (from interviews or testing), your decision and the reason. The log is useful later in your pilot proposal, because it shows what verifiers have already contributed.

Some requests need care.

- **A request that weakens quality.** For example, a verifier asks for a pass mark to be lowered. Decline, and explain the effect on the credential's meaning.
- **A request that suits only one verifier.** Decide whether to create a variant for that verifier or to keep one version. Many variants are costly to maintain.
- **A request outside your control.** Pass it to whoever owns it, such as an awarding body.

Co-design builds relationships. Verifiers who have shaped a credential tend to understand it better. Even so, involvement does not guarantee recognition, so do not treat it as an agreement.

### Worked example and practice

**Worked example: Co-designing an evidence page with a hiring manager and a talent lead**

At Meridian Foods, Amara has completed interviews about "Line leader readiness". Plant managers told her that they trust observed performance and distrust course attendance. The talent team lead said the HR system holds job profiles with a list of required competencies, and that credentials which map to those competencies would be easier to use.

Amara frames a design question: "How can the credential show observed performance in a form that matches our job profile competencies?"

She invites two plant managers and the talent team lead to a 90-minute session. She brings a one-page draft of the credential description. They sketch two versions. In the first, the description lists the four assessed tasks and the observer's role. In the second, each task is tied to a named competency in the team leader job profile.

She tests both versions on a sample earner. A plant manager reads version two and says, "I can see straight away which competencies were observed, and who observed them." The talent lead asks whether the assessor's name could be shown. A manager asks for the pass mark to be lowered so more people qualify. Amara declines and explains that the pass mark reflects the safety-critical handover task. She records the request and her reason in the change log.

They agree on version two with an added field for assessor role. Amara notes that the link to job profile competencies applies at Meridian Foods and may need review for suppliers.

## Pilot agreements

**In this module you will**

- explain why a pilot is a sensible first step,
- define scope, terms and measures for a pilot recognition arrangement,
- identify risks and decision rules before you start,
- draft a pilot proposal that a verifier can agree to.

### Why start with a pilot

A pilot recognition arrangement is a small, time-limited agreement in which a verifier tries out recognising a specific credential in a defined way. It lets both sides learn before they commit more widely.

A pilot is useful because it lowers the risk for the verifier. They are asked to try, not to promise. It also gives you real evidence about what works, which is more convincing than a presentation.

A pilot is not a final partnership. Say so clearly, so that nobody believes the credential is now accepted everywhere. At the end, the parties decide whether to stop, adjust, extend or scale.

A pilot recognition arrangement can take several forms.

- **Interview or shortlist use.** A hiring team agrees to consider the credential as one of several pieces of evidence when shortlisting for a defined role.
- **Internal mobility use.** A talent team accepts the credential as an entry requirement for an internal development programme.
- **Credit or entry.** An education provider agrees to grant defined credit or exemption to earners who meet set conditions.
- **Association recognition.** A professional body agrees to count the credential towards a membership or development requirement.

In each case, the verifier keeps their own decision rights. The pilot defines how the credential is considered, not that it overrides existing rules.

Choose a pilot that is small enough to start soon and large enough to teach you something. A pilot with five earners and one team may be right. A pilot that depends on a board meeting six months away may be too slow.

Check whether any rules limit what the verifier can agree. These might be internal policies, collective agreements with employee representatives, equal treatment duties, data protection obligations or the rules of a regulator or awarding body. <!-- VERIFY: Advise learners to check applicable local laws, regulations and organisational policies with their own legal or compliance advisers; no specific legal provisions are cited here. --> The verifier's own compliance or legal staff should be consulted early. You should not give legal advice yourself.

<details><summary>Facilitator notes</summary>

**Live element (about 30 minutes).** Discuss in small groups: "What is the smallest pilot that would teach you whether this credential is useful to your highest-priority verifier?" Groups share their answers and the facilitator highlights the common constraints raised.

</details>

### Terms of a pilot

A pilot agreement should be short and clear. Aim for two to four pages. It describes what each party will do, for how long and how you will judge the result.

A strong pilot proposal covers these elements.

1. **Purpose.** One or two sentences: what are we trying out and why?
2. **Parties and roles.** Who is the issuer, who is the verifier, who is the contact on each side and who decides?
3. **Credential covered.** The name, version, assessment standard and what it evidences.
4. **Recognition arrangement.** What exactly will the verifier do? For example, "treat the credential as meeting criterion 3 of the role profile" or "grant exemption from module X". State what the arrangement does not do.
5. **Scope.** Which roles, programmes, sites or cohorts? How many earners? Set a limit.
6. **Duration.** Start date, end date and review dates. Pilots often last three to twelve months, depending on hiring or teaching cycles.
7. **Evidence and verification.** How will the verifier check the credential, and what will the issuer provide? Refer to what you know from ICB-F04 about what a verification check shows.
8. **Responsibilities.** What the issuer will do (for example, respond to queries, keep records, notify about revocations) and what the verifier will do (for example, apply the arrangement as agreed, share agreed data).
9. **Data and privacy.** What information is shared, with whose consent, for what purpose and how it is protected. Agree this with the people responsible for data protection.
10. **Success measures.** What would show that the pilot worked? Choose measures both sides can observe, such as time taken to check evidence, number of earners accepted, verifier confidence ratings and earner feedback. Do not set targets you cannot justify.
11. **Risks and safeguards.** What could go wrong and how will you respond? Include a way to pause or stop.
12. **Review and next steps.** When will you review, who attends, and what decisions are possible?
13. **Costs.** Who pays for what, if anything.
14. **Communications.** Who may say what about the pilot, and when.

Write numbers in the proposal as agreed targets or limits, and label example figures in your drafts as illustrative until the parties agree real ones.

A pilot proposal is not always a legal contract. Whether you need a formal contract, a memorandum of understanding or a simple exchange of emails depends on the parties and their rules. Ask the verifier what they usually use. <!-- VERIFY: Whether a formal contract is required depends on the parties' own procedures and applicable law; check with advisers. -->

### Measuring, deciding and avoiding common problems

Agree how you will decide before the pilot starts. This avoids arguments later.

**Measures.** Pick a few measures, not a long list.

- **Use.** How often was the credential considered under the pilot?
- **Effort.** How long did verification and decision take compared with the usual process?
- **Confidence.** How confident did the verifier feel in the evidence, on a simple scale?
- **Outcomes.** What happened to earners considered under the arrangement, as far as the parties can agree to record? Take care with claims. A few cases do not show that a credential predicts job performance.
- **Experience.** What did earners and verifiers say about the process?

**Decision rules.** Write down, in advance, what would lead to each outcome.

- **Extend or scale:** the measures meet the agreed thresholds and no serious problems arose.
- **Adjust:** the arrangement is promising, but specific changes are needed.
- **Stop:** the arrangement does not meet the verifier's needs or creates unacceptable risk.

**Common problems and responses.**

- **A champion leaves.** Record the arrangement in the verifier's own documents and name a second contact.
- **Low take-up.** Check whether earners and verifiers know about the arrangement. Check whether the process is too hard.
- **Misunderstanding of scope.** Review the "what this does not do" section and share a one-page summary with all staff involved.
- **Earner complaints.** Agree a route for questions and appeals before the start. Ensure that earners who are not recognised are told why.
- **Changes to the credential during the pilot.** Agree a rule: no changes to assessment standards without notice to the verifier.

Share results honestly, including what did not work. A well-run pilot that ends in "stop" is still a useful outcome, because both sides learn and can avoid wider costs.

## Assessment and badge

### The assessed task

You complete one assessed task in three parts, using the same credential throughout the course.

1. **Verifier map.** One to two pages. A list of at least 10 verifiers by role or team, each with the decision they make, a placement on an influence and interest map with a one-line reason, and a selection of 4 to 6 to engage first with a short justification.
2. **Interview summary.** Two pages. | A. Verifier identification | 1 | Verifiers listed mostly as broad labels; few decisions named | At least 10 verifiers named by role or team, each with the decision they make | Also includes unlikely but plausible verifiers and shows how verifiers influence one another |
| B. Prioritisation | 1 | Placement on the map without reasons, or selection not justified | Each placement has a one-line reason; 4 to 6 verifiers chosen with a sound justification | Marks assumptions to test, links choices to the credential's purpose and revises the map after research |
| C. Interview design and conduct | 2 | Questions are mostly closed, leading or hypothetical | Open questions about past behaviour and evidence; 3 to 5 interviews with different verifiers, role-play stated honestly | Interviews uncover decision rights, distrusted evidence and format needs; interviewer adapts to answers |
| D. Interview summary quality | 2 | Notes listed without synthesis; findings not labelled | Seven parts present; findings labelled by strength of evidence; implications stated | Distinguishes patterns from single views, notes disagreements and updates the verifier map |
| E. Co-design | 3 | Changes listed without evidence or reasons | Design question, tested draft and change log with at least five entries showing accept, adapt, decline or pass on | Protects the credential's quality, handles conflicting requests and records what stays fixed with reasons |

Pilot terms | 4 | Several of the 14 elements missing or unclear; limits not stated | All elements present; scope, duration, responsibilities and limits are clear; numbers labelled illustrative | Terms are realistic for the verifier's rules, include a named second contact and a clear route for questions and appeals |

### Pass rule

To earn the badge you need all of the following:

- a score of at least 2 on every criterion A to H, and
- evidence submitted for all three parts: verifier map, interview summary and pilot proposal, and
- completion of the final knowledge check below (the knowledge check supports your learning and is not marked by the assessor).

If one or two criteria score 1, the assessor gives written feedback and you may revise and resubmit once. If your submission lacks one of the three evidence parts, it cannot be assessed.

### Evidence submitted for the badge

- Verifier map
- Interview summary (with change log attached)
- Pilot proposal

The badge is "Engaging Verifiers and Employers (Ability, L3)", alignment code ICSF-ECOS-L3-A. The badge shows that you practised engaging verifiers and setting up a pilot recognition arrangement in a controlled setting and met the rubric.

### Final knowledge check

These questions cover all four learning outcomes. Choose one answer for each.

**Question 1.** (Outcome 1) You are preparing a verifier list for a new micro-credential. Which approach gives you the most useful list?

[( )] List broad categories such as employers, universities and regulators
[(X)] Name specific roles or teams, the decision each makes about earners, and how they would view the credential
[( )] List only organisations that have already shown interest
[( )] List the competitors of your issuing organisation
[[?]] Can you contact the entry and ask them something?
****
Specific roles or teams, with the decisions they make, can be contacted and interviewed. Broad categories cannot, and limiting the list to interested organisations hides important verifiers.
****

**Question 2.** (Outcome 1) A professional body has high influence over entry to a licence but shows little interest in your credential. What is the best plan?

[( )] Ignore it until it shows interest
[( )] Engage it first with a full proposal
[(X)] Keep it satisfied: find out what would make the credential relevant to it and avoid surprises
[( )] Remove it from the map
[[?]] High influence, lower interest.
****
Verifiers with high influence and lower interest should be kept satisfied. Ignoring them risks blocks later, and a full proposal before you understand their needs is premature.
****

**Question 3.** (Outcome 1) Why should placements on an influence and interest map be revisited after interviews?

[( )] Because maps expire after a fixed time
[( )] Because verifiers ask to be moved
[(X)] Because the placements are judgements and interviews give you evidence that may change them
[( )] Because the map is part of the credential
[[?]] Placements are not facts.
****
Your first placements rest on assumptions. Interviews can show who really decides and who cares, so the map should be updated.
****

**Question 4.** (Outcome 2) Which interview opening is most likely to uncover what evidence a hiring manager trusts?

[( )] "Would you say credentials are important for hiring?"
[( )] "Let me tell you about our credential and its benefits."
[(X)] "Tell me about the last time you filled this kind of role. What did you look at, and what did you trust most?"
[( )] "Do you plan to use digital credentials next year?"
[[?]] Ask about a real, recent occasion.
****
Questions about the last real occasion give concrete detail about evidence and trust. The others are leading, a pitch or hypothetical.
****

**Question 5.** (Outcome 2) In your interview summary, one verifier said a board must approve new credit types. How should you record it?

[( )] As a fact that applies to all education providers
[( )] Leave it out because only one person said it
[(X)] As heard from one verifier, with a plan to test it in other interviews
[( )] As a legal requirement
[[?]] Be clear about how many people said it.
****
A single mention is a lead. Recording it with its source strength, and planning to test it, keeps your summary honest and useful.
****

**Question 6.** (Outcome 3) During co-design, a verifier asks you to lower a pass mark on a safety-critical task so that more people qualify. What do you do?

[( )] Agree, to build the relationship
[( )] Change it only for that verifier without telling others
[(X)] Decline, explain how the pass mark protects the credential's meaning and record the request and your reason in the change log
[( )] Stop the co-design work
[[?]] The issuer keeps responsibility for quality.
****
Lowering the pass mark would weaken the credential's meaning. Declining with an explanation, and recording it, protects quality and keeps trust.
****

**Question 7.** (Outcome 3) What is the best way to test a revised credential description with a verifier?

[( )] Ask whether they like the design
[( )] Send it by email and wait for a reply
[(X)] Ask them to use it on a realistic sample earner and watch what they look at and ask
[( )] Present the assessment statistics only
[[?]] Observe use, not opinion.
****
Using the draft on a realistic case shows whether it supports a decision. Opinions about appearance do not.
****

**Question 8.** (Outcome 4) Which statement belongs in a pilot proposal?

[( )] "Earners holding this credential will perform well in the role."
[(X)] "The pilot covers up to 20 earners (illustrative) for six months. It does not override existing selection rules and does not guarantee an interview."
[( )] "The verifier agrees to accept the credential permanently."
[( )] "No review is needed during the pilot."
[[?]] State scope and limits.
****
A pilot states scope, duration and what the arrangement does not do. Promises of performance, permanent acceptance or no review cannot be supported.
****

**Question 9.** (Outcome 4) Why write decision rules for extend, adjust and stop before the pilot starts?

[( )] To make the verifier commit to extending
[(X)] To make the judgement of results fair and avoid disputes about what success means
[( )] To prevent any changes to the credential
[( )] To delay the start of the pilot
[[?]] Think about mixed results.
****
Agreed rules stop either side from reading results to suit itself and give the verifier a clear exit if needed.
****

**Question 10.** (Outcome 4) Who should you involve early to check whether policy or legal limits affect a pilot?

[( )] Only your own marketing team
[( )] Earners only
[(X)] The verifier's compliance, legal or HR staff, and relevant advisers
[( )] Nobody, as pilots are informal
[[?]] The verifier works within their own rules.
****
The verifier must act within their policies and applicable rules. Early involvement of their compliance or legal staff avoids proposals that cannot be accepted. You should not give legal advice yourself.
****
